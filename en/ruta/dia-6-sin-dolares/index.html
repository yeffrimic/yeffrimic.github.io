Chapter 6 · Day 6 · 30 Oct 2026

We officially ran out of dollars

San Miguel → Chinandega · Nicaragua · 270 km traveled

We left San Miguel around 11 a.m. The plan was to go through San Lorenzo, in Honduras, have lunch, visit the port and find a spot nearby to ride. There's not much to highlight in this part, other than that, sure enough, only El Salvador has a marked bike lane.

We reached the El Salvador–Honduras border around 1 or 2 p.m. Here you can see José waiting in line at immigration. Interestingly, El Salvador doesn't stamp passports on entry or exit, they just give you little tickets, and it's always nerve-racking that they take them from you, because you have no idea what's going on with that. So from now on we'll take photos of every slip of paper they give us, to keep a record.

We crossed to the Honduran side and, total newbies, we didn't see anyone stopping us or anything, hahahaha. I told José: "No way, there has to be something that shows we entered and that we're leaving." So we asked. He went into a pretty nice building and texts me: "Yep, they're stamping, I'm in line," hahahaha. I parked behind a pickup, we got out and waited in line for about 10 minutes… only to realize we were in the exit line, HAHAHA, not the entry line. We went to the other side and got stamped: fingerprints, photo, "what do you do for a living?", the usual process, super efficient. They didn't inspect the car or anything. I have to say that at the Salvadoran border they did stop us and check the car, but in Honduras they didn't.

We continued toward San Lorenzo. There was a really nice straight stretch and we decided to ride it; you can see the video here. Like last time, we realized it's more dangerous, for the car and for the rider, to stay behind the whole time, especially on a road full of semis. So José got out and started pedaling, and I drove ahead almost every kilometer so I wouldn't be in the way. There were climbs, descents and scorching heat, until we decided to put the bike away, after about 5 km of pedaling. The fatigue of these days is starting to show. And that's even though we rested in San Salvador… but we didn't really rest: I went and got stuck in traffic and so did José, haha. So honestly, we haven't worked as we should these days.

Leaving José behind, I drove ahead and there was a police checkpoint, hahahaha. The officer asks for my license and vehicle registration card, which of course I carry digitally, because who has the physical one? And he says: "Ahh, look, haha, you're not wearing a seatbelt, I'm going to give you a little ticket," hahahaha. And I'm like: "Come on, officer, don't be like that. I just got in the car about 100 meters ago, I'm keeping an eye on my friend on the bike." And there I was: "Don't be a buzzkill, officer," hahahaha.

Then José arrives, and the officer says: "Ahh, but don't you have any water there?" And I'm like: "Nope, but I can give you an electrolyte drink," haha. "No, water, water." And I said: "Haha, no, officer, sorry, we don't even have cash," HAHAHA, me guarding my 50 cents. In the end he let me go; he just said: "Put your seatbelt on, then."

After that there was some urgency, because we'd left the lodging a bit late that day and it was a 5-hour drive. We finally reached San Lorenzo, but it was already 3 p.m., so I told José: "Look, I don't want to drive at night." I've had bad experiences driving at night in Guatemala: unmarked roads, people with stadium floodlights on their cars that blind you. So better sooner than later, HAHAHA.

We found a really interesting place in a shopping center. Shopping centers are very different in each country: you're used to enclosed places with tons of stores, and here they're more like open plazas around the parking lot. "Las Delicias del Puerto", or something like that, was the name. Given how worn out he was, I suggested José have some soup to recover: he had chicken soup, and I had a beef stew that was really good. We ate in 10 minutes and headed for the El Guasaule border. Coming into Choluteca we found a beautiful bridge; you can see it in the video. In Honduras they did stamp our passports, so the collection keeps growing.

We finally reached the border and the nightmare began. We passed the Honduran checkpoint, all good, all correct, and when we entered the Nicaraguan checkpoint they stopped us and asked for our papers. The thing is, they wanted the vehicle registration card printed, haha. PRIIINTED! Otherwise we couldn't cross. And we were like: "But where can we print around here?" "Over on the other side, ask for help." And they sent us back to the Honduran side.

As we drove up the bridge there were several police officers, and one of them stopped us and said: "You're going fast, eh, you're going fast." And I said: "No, officer, sorry if it seemed that way, but we're going slow." And she repeated it like three times, HAHAHA.

We went back to the Honduran side and drove around until we found (here you can see it) a place with a printer in the middle of nowhere. The map showed a gas station nearby, so we went there first, supposedly to get lempiras to pay for the copy, HAHA. That gas station was more abandoned than Guatemala's roads and there was nothing, so we said: "Well, even if they charge us around 100 quetzales to print the registration card, that's what we get for being idiots," HAHAHA.

I hadn't told you, but when we got to the border there was a line of trucks almost 1 km long waiting to cross. I had to drive against traffic to get through, because the trucks weren't going to move until 6 p.m., but light vehicles could pass, so, well, hahahaha. We found the copy shop and parked between two trucks. We asked the lady if she took dollars and she said yes. She printed the card and charged us our last 50 cents, HAHAHA. José pulled a dollar out of his backpack and she gave him back 15 lempiras, which is about 50 cents. So, officially, we ran out of the first and only 5 dollars we'd been carrying until that day.

We went back to the border, which took us about 20 or 30 minutes. While we waited in line to enter Nicaragua, several people who informally help folks who are lost like us, hahahaha, told us there were better ways to skip the trucks: one was to go another way and cross the river, haha, and another was to drive up on the sidewalk, HAHAHAHA, because it was wide enough for small cars. But we didn't want to risk it, because, again, this is a long-term process: if something on the car broke just to get 20 meters ahead, it wasn't worth it.

We arrived, crossed the bridge and passed the first inspection. I tell the officer: "We're back from printing." And she says: "Ahh, I'm hungry, you don't have any food on you?" HAHAHAHAHAHAHAHA. She laughed, checked everything and let us through. It's really interesting how the same process, without help or guidance, can take so long.

Before getting to immigration we drove around lost, because it wasn't clear where we were supposed to go with the car or where the office was. In the end someone helped us and we got there. They asked for our passports, reason for the visit and phone numbers, and took our photos and fingerprints, as always. Then we had to register the car, but I had no idea how or where, so I asked about everything. To make a long story short: the car process took an hour and a half, we had to buy insurance, and that was it.

It was already dark, so we were going a bit slow. We got to Chinandega around 8 p.m., and here too, haha: José's friend wanted to take us to a place that was packed. Between one thing and another, we dropped the car off at the hotel and got to where we'd been invited to eat. And look how good it was, hahahaha: it looked more like lunch than dinner. Nicaraguan cheese is incredible, so beautiful, so delicious, haha. We talked for a good while, went back to the hotel and realized we were exhausted. That's why I'm only writing the blog today.

Chinandega is a pretty nice city. We're going to have to invent a term for this kind of city. In Guatemala you arrive in small towns that have their own magic, a certain identity, not only because of the Indigenous peoples who bring them color, but because of their customs. Here, in Chinandega, in San Lorenzo and even in San Miguel, the cities are big, but I think since we were just passing through, we didn't stay long enough for them to really win us over. Don't get me wrong: it's nice, really nice. And I'm surprised that, sure enough, we have the worst roads in Central America, HAHAHAHA.

But the cities are all quite similar, which is great, because it means we have more in common and you feel good everywhere. Nobody ever looked down on us anywhere. The heat, though… but that's another story.

Today, on day seven, we're going to rest, work and do what we set out to do last week. We'll keep you posted on how it goes.

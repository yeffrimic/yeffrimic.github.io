Chapter 8 · Day 8 · 1 Nov 2026

Improvising cost us big time

Chinandega → Managua · Nicaragua · 135 km traveled

Damn the moment I thought all of Central America would be the same. Running into reality: in other countries things work and people actually follow the rules. For whatever reason, but they follow them.

One of the things that frustrates me most is driving along the roads of Central American countries that, through Guatemalan eyes, are always supposed to be worse than ours, and realizing that when a government, a State, doesn't abandon its infrastructure and its roads, it helps not only its own country but foreign investment too. But in Guatemala nobody cares about anything beyond what's right in front of them: if I leave for work at 9, I don't care about traffic, because it's already over, or I leave later to avoid it. I don't care about the people who leave in the morning, because it doesn't happen to me. Damn the tunnel vision we have, damn the local mindset and the selfishness we live in, in Guatemala.

You have no idea how much it hurt to see smaller cities with better infrastructure, better development and more order than our second city, Xela. There's no comparison, none at all. And in Guatemala we're proud of Xela, me first. But once again I realize we're used to mediocrity and to applauding the attempts at cities we have. It's incredible to see that El Salvador has a city with beautiful roads in the east of the country, and we have Chiquimula, which barely has one shopping center and probably not even elevators. I just remembered, haha, that a few years ago there was a news report about the first elevator in Totonicapán. Come on, hahahaha.

Anyway, after all that frustration, we left Chinandega for León, a beautiful city. On the way we came across a police checkpoint, where they very kindly told us we were missing an insurance policy that's mandatory for everyone. And I thought: "Wow, that's what they tried to bring in in Guatemala." But back home, as with everything, we're a bunch of mediocre people who don't want anything, and the authorities have always been weak, because staying in power comes before looking out for the common good.

The police officer asked me to get out of the car and explained how the country works: that it's best to follow the rules, not to drive faster than 50 km/h because the fines are high and, above all, that they take your license so you come in and pay. By then I was scared and close to tears, because once again I was improvising, once again out of ignorance, but this time as an adult and in another country, where ignorance of the law is no excuse. We talked and he gave me the chance to go to the next city to find an insurance company. He just said: "It's Sunday and tomorrow's a holiday, so I think it'll be hard. I'd advise you not to drive without that insurance, because they'll take your license." I was thinking: they have every right to do it, even though nobody ever told us we needed that insurance. And a correction: on day 6 I told you we bought insurance at the border. Well, it turns out what we paid for was the vehicle fumigation, HAHAHA, not the insurance. Proof that we make mistakes too.

We got to León and, at the speed they told us to drive, it took almost two hours, hahahaha. We stopped at a shopping center and everything was closed; then at the central park and, again, everything was closed. That's when I realized that, just like in Xela, almost everything closes on Sundays and holidays. Until one of José's friends saved us with the contact of a lady who works on Sundays.

Once I know I've done things wrong, it always weighs on me, because they're avoidable. I didn't want to eat, I was down, and the thought came back: you're not in your country. If they take your license, you don't even have cash, and we can't fall that far behind: one day late is one more expensive day, and the plans fall apart, not only mine, but José's and everyone we're going to see.

In the end we had some meat skewers that were really good. I didn't take photos of León's historic center, which has about a thousand churches, HAHAHA. Here's a map so you can see them.

Map of León's churches →

What I forgot to tell you, and why there are banknotes in the photos: we had lunch at a restaurant called La Basílica, which, sure enough, is near the Basilica, hahahaha. We asked if they took cards and they said yes. Then, once we'd been served, they told us they didn't have a card machine and we had to pay cash or by bank transfer, haha. Walking around we found a BAC branch, which was closed, and a LAFISE one, a bank I'd never heard of. I had to walk about 4 blocks to the BAC ATM, which wouldn't let me withdraw, and then went to LAFISE, where I finally took out about 2,000 córdobas… and they charged us 10 dollars for the transaction. So here's a tip: make one big withdrawal so the fee hurts less, or find an ATM that's compatible with your card so you don't pay a commission.

We ate and then got lost, haha, because Waze sends us wherever it wants and only God and the map know where we're going. We got to where they had to give us the insurance receipt, paid, and kept going, confident we'd have no more problems, hoping they wouldn't stop us again, haha, but now with our papers in order. Then we started researching what we need to enter Costa Rica, and it's the same procedure: fumigation, paying the mandatory vehicle insurance and registering what you're carrying.

From León to Managua, let me tell you: the roads are incredible. There was a beautiful plain where José rode the bike. We're getting better, and it's really interesting to see the curve: more kilometers at first, then fewer, and now we're going up every day. Once again, everything we're going through on this trip is incredible.

Now we do it like this: José rides ahead for about 10 minutes, then I catch up in the car, pass him and drive a couple of kilometers ahead; then he passes me, and so on. But on the last pass there was nowhere to park, so I went way ahead, almost 10 km, haha. And José rode every single one of them: about 10 to 15 km in total. I'm really proud of him.

We reached Managua on another big, clean, orderly road, with people driving at a sensible speed and respecting, if not all, most of the signs. No traffic, everything flowing.

Looking for the hotel, I realized the street looked familiar, and it turns out we'd already stayed at that hotel in 2019. It's quite nice and comfortable. 70 dollars for 3 nights, with a pool and buffet breakfast included. Pretty good.

Honestly, after all the hustle, getting into the pool helped me slow down. We ate with José and went to bed early. We were tired, because it's not just the pedaling or the driving: it's knowing we're not in Guatemala and that anything we do even slightly wrong can cost us more.

Someone once told me: "You guys are traveling to get away from stress." And I'm thinking: there's more stress in what we're doing right now than in lying around at home, with my family, food, a roof, internet and everything covered. There was no need to leave, but there is a need to keep moving and to keep being the best version of myself.

I also discovered I want a king-size bed in my room. Even if it doesn't fit where I'll be living right now, I want it and I'm going to buy it, haha. Even if I barely use it, because I can see I'll be traveling more in the coming months and years, I want it, because how nice to have such a big space.

I've been thinking about a lot of things, putting them in perspective and reflecting a lot on how I do things, but I'll tell you about that in the coming days. It's incredible that we've already spent a week outside the country, taking on a challenge we set almost as a joke, and that neither of us got scared. Here we are, committed to the cause. And it's nice to know I have the support of someone who's just as motivated and committed to the idea, or more, and who also dreams of making things happen. Finally I'm not the only one leading: we take turns solving and leading, and that makes it even better.

I hope this one wasn't too harsh, but I am angry about the whole situation: seeing that in Guatemala we rested on our laurels, proud, living in the shadow of what we once were, while others now surpass us. The problem isn't that there are better countries; it's that they lie to us, telling us we're the best, to keep us just as mediocre. See you in the next blog. Love you all.

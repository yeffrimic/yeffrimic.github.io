Chapter 15 · Day 15 · 8 Nov 2026

Wait, McDonald's in Costa Rica sells rice and beans?

Liberia → Puntarenas · Costa Rica · 135 km traveled

Today we got up early, like every day; that is, me before 9 and José at 6 or 7 in the morning HAHAHA. The night before we'd been talking about the cultural differences between the Central American countries. As I said in other posts, we're more alike than we like to admit, but some things are very distinct, and one of them is gallo pinto (rice and beans). The marketing says gallo pinto is Costa Rican, but I think I saw more gallo pinto in Nicaragua than in what we've seen of Costa Rica so far.

We'd been spoiled: the last few days we had breakfast at HC Liri, in Nicaragua, which is a 10 out of 10 in everything. They make really good breakfasts and, above all, some tomatoes with salt and pepper that, I don't know if it was the tomato or what, but they were amazing. Today, on the other hand, the only breakfast we could find was fast food, so we ended up at McDonald's HAHAHAHA.

And there it was. Costa Ricans and Nicaraguans have been arguing for years over who gallo pinto belongs to, and meanwhile McDonald's shows up and slaps its brand on it: the McPinto. Rice and beans, but from McDonald's HAHAHAHA. I still can't believe it, and you know what the worst part is? It was good. The eggs with those little sausage patties they put in the muffins were pretty tasty.

And since today was more about eating than pedaling, we're launching a rating system: the Adas. Ada is my kitty, the same one who served as the measuring stick for the fish in San Juan del Sur, and from now on every place we eat gets 1 to 5 Adas:

🐱 Better stay away.
🐱🐱 If there's nothing else.
🐱🐱🐱 It does the job.
🐱🐱🐱🐱 Worth it.
🐱🐱🐱🐱🐱 You have to go, no excuses.

🐱🐱🐱 McDonald's (McPinto): 3 out of 5 Adas. Not a place to come to Costa Rica for, but the McPinto deserves the photo.

We left our lodging at 11 heading to Puntarenas. It's been cloudy and stormy these days: it rained at night, and part of the morning in Liberia too. Waze said 90 km, an hour and a half. We set off and once again noticed the difference in the roads; sorry, but I'm still impressed by Nicaragua's HAHAHA. A friend on Facebook told me he did this same trip 10 years ago and that even back then Nicaragua had the best roads in Central America.

After the first hour we ran into a small, tiiiiiiiny stretch of about 30 km under reconstruction. We'd move a bit, stop for 10 minutes, move a bit more. We were both glued to Waze:

—What does it say?
—An hour forty.
—What do you mean an hour forty? We've already done 30 km.

We had no idea what we'd gotten ourselves into HAHAHAHA.

Well, as it turns out, they're rebuilding the road from Peñas Blancas to San José, and on top of that it was raining pretty hard. So yes, Waze was right: a trip that looked like lunch in Puntarenas ended with us arriving at Código Beach Club almost at 4 in the afternoon. I told José the traffic didn't bother me knowing it would be worth it. And it was: I was stuffed.

I ordered shrimp pasta al ajillo (garlic), delicious, and this time José got a fried fish: a GIGANTIC red snapper, the size of the two we ate in Nicaragua put together HAHAHA. (Ada, if you're reading this: this fish was bigger than you).

1/3

🐱🐱🐱🐱🐱 Código Beach Club: 5 out of 5 Adas. A must in Puntarenas.

We finished lunch and went straight to the hotel. The whole road shows the sea and, as I told you before, the most impressive thing is that the mountain drops straight into the beach, so the salty smell and the beach weather feel very different from Guatemala's beaches.

At night we went out for a walk, and something that pleasantly surprises me and that I really like is that people here really take over public spaces and make them their own. It seems the municipality of Puntarenas, at least in this area, has done a good job (I'll have to ask the locals). They have a boardwalk and several really pretty piers for taking photos, playgrounds for kids and, most amazing of all, whole families going out on a Sunday to eat or have a drink, bringing their own beach chairs because obviously they're more comfortable than the benches. There were tourists, yes, but mostly families, and that really impressed me.

1/2

We had dinner at El Chicharrón del Coco. The chicharrón (pork crackling) tacos, 10 out of 10. The shrimp ceviche wasn't for us: we don't know if it's a local recipe, but it was just a bit of liquid, a few chopped vegetables and shrimp straight out of the fridge.

🐱🐱🐱🐱 El Chicharrón del Coco: 4 out of 5 Adas. The tacos earn all five; the ceviche takes one away.

Watching those families sitting by the sea got me thinking. It's been 15 days since we left and there's still a lot to discover, but my heart feels very grateful, because I'm starting to stop thinking locally and start thinking regionally. Hopefully soon I'll start thinking globally. Letting go of local thinking removes the limit of distance: moving 100, 200 or 1,200 km is no longer a problem, even if it's just to keep going.

That already shows in my projects. On November 27 we have Arduino Day in Antigua Guatemala; it'll be exhausting, but we'll pull it off. Traveling removes your limits, reminds you how fleeting things are and how small our problems are. Going out into the world doesn't just help you reflect: it makes you realize there are many more things out there to do, discover, eat and see, and many more options and points of view than the ones we have now.

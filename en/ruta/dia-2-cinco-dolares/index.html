We went to El Salvador with five dollars

El Cerinal, Barberena → Termaland, Ahuachapán · El Salvador · 97 km traveled

We left El Cerinal at 8 a.m. and stopped in Barberena to go to Dollar City, hoping to find a replacement for the mount that broke, the one that holds the phone on the bike. No luck: Dollar City doesn't sell that kind of thing. Right then my grandfather called me: "You forgot something with batteries… and did you leave the drone?" So we had to go back, but it wasn't a big deal, because Barberena and El Cerinal are close.

After that we decided to make a short hop in the car. The Barberena bypass, like the rest of the highway, is very dangerous: parts of the right lane are in terrible shape and, on top of that, the cars go way too fast. So we drove past Cuilapa and started pedaling from there. The goal for the day was the Los Esclavos Bridge which, as the story goes, was finished by the devil himself, and it's still standing more than 400 years after it was built.

This stretch was a bit easier, because the descent was pretty steep and José barely had to pedal. But what goes down must come up. At the end of the climb José got off and walked those two kilometers. I stopped to wait for him, but it was very dangerous with all the trucks and semis, so I went ahead and waited for him at a pull-out. A few minutes later he arrived, tired and fed up: the weight of the bike, plus the discomfort of riding along the curb and then walking with the bike beside him, gave him an injury. But that didn't stop him from reaching the bridge. There's a small roundabout before it, so we stopped there for a bit while he recovered, and then kept going until we finally reached Los Esclavos. Goal of the day: done, about 10 km pedaled from Cuilapa.

From there on we went by car. All that was left was getting to Jalpatagua to have lunch and work. It's interesting how the towns keep changing and how, depending on what's around, people behave. It got me wondering what comes first: the business, or the people who live there with out-of-the-box ideas.

We had lunch and headed for Ahuachapán, to a hotel called Termaland. We crossed the border, and it's really interesting to see the contrasts between each country, but also the similarities. On the other side we got a nice highway and then, haha, before we knew it, it was dark and we'd missed two turnoffs to Termaland. As it turns out, Termaland sits in the middle of a rough country road. After missing the second turnoff we stopped at the next gas station and saw that my route sent us one way and José's another. We took a leap of faith, hoping only the entrance would give us a scare. Nope: it was about 5 km of pure dirt road, where we found several small cars asking for help, because getting there really was rough. No houses nearby, no streetlights, nothing.

We had to go slowly and, after cursing the whole way, hahaha, we arrived at a paradise, where you could hear every frog around and see the now nearly extinct fireflies. They welcomed us, showed us the room, very nice by the way, and above all told us there was no restaurant available, HAHAHAHA. And we'd said "we'll eat when we get there." We had no idea where we were going to end up, much less that there'd be no food.

The guy tells us: "Oh sure, sometimes there are pupusas down there," haha. They were fairly cheap: six pupusas revueltas for 3 dollars. The funniest part is that we hadn't exchanged any dollars, because we figured we wouldn't need them, haha, and by pure chance José had 5 dollars on him, which were going to cover dinner. Then the guy says: "Right outside, on the left, in the first house, there's a little shop; you can at least buy something there." So, no pupusas. Off we went.

Out here in the backcountry of El Salvador we arrived at a humble little house with a tiny shop that had almost nothing. Just instant soups and snacks, the little bags of chips and candy. We had 5 dollars and I felt awkward because I didn't know how much things cost, so we asked for two soups, which ended up costing 90 cents, two Kolashampan sodas to fit in, and two bags of Tostecas. Since I'm not shy, I asked the lady if she could spare us some hot water to make the soups. She didn't understand me, until she did, haha, and they made the soups for us at no extra charge. In the end, dinner cost us 2.90 dollars. We went to El Salvador with five dollars and now we only have two, HAHAHAHA.

We started chatting and the lady told us it used to be really dangerous around here, but that it stopped being so a few years ago, and that people come when the weather's good. And I'm thinking: that morning, in Guatemala, we couldn't go to Laguna del Pino because my grandfather advised us not to go so early and to go on a weekend instead: on weekdays, gangs make it very dangerous, especially for us with a drone and all. And now here we are, somewhere super dark and remote, and they tell us it's super safe, HAHAHA.

The lady told us she loved going to Guatemala, that it used to be cheaper and that she really liked it. But what I want to highlight is that, incredibly, she and her family spent almost an hour chatting with us. That said, one of her relatives, a boy of about 16, came out to keep her company and stayed close to us as a way of protecting her. It's amazing how, in the end, you trust strangers: us in them, and them in us. It all felt pretty comfortable, but it's the kind of situation that could turn dangerous for anyone.

To end the day we got into the hotel's hot springs pools to rest and be ready for tomorrow: heading to Santa Ana walking, pedaling or running. We'll go to the waterfall and see what else to do. Thanks for reading this far; I hope you keep reading and following along on this journey. Please share. Love you all.

It's never the same river

Managua · Nicaragua

Yesterday was the Day of the Dead in Guatemala, and José was telling me it's a very Latin American date, because it was a holiday even in Nicaragua. That made me think about how lucky we were to find a place open on Sunday, because on Monday we obviously wouldn't have found anything: the holiday was moved to Monday.

At the end of day 8 I was scrolling social media: lots of people went to the cemetery to visit their loved ones. I also saw something that always gets me thinking: how we take cultural expressions from other peoples and make them ours as if we'd always had them. I think it has to do with identity, with age and with that stage in adolescence when we reject our roots because they seem old-fashioned or aren't popular, and embrace faraway customs instead.

I saw Guatemalans with Mexican altars, or celebrating Halloween. Everyone has the right to do what they want, but I wish we knew the background of those customs, which weren't made to look pretty in photos, but to honor our ancestors and loved ones who have passed. We have our own traditions here too: going to the cemetery, flying giant kites and decorating the graves. I hope we don't set them aside for whatever's trending.

This was a day of a lot of thinking; we really didn't do anything. Day 8, after so much stress, knocked us out. I spent the whole morning sleeping, so much that I was late for the breakfast buffet. José had already eaten, and by pure luck I grabbed some fruit and a couple of brownies and said: that's enough. In Nicaragua the portions are big, extremely big, of everything, and compared with prices and portions in Guatemala it's usually cheap, sooo cheap that we get lost in the conversion and always end up overpaying, HAHAHAHA.

For lunch we went to Tip-Top, because apparently we've made it our mission to try every fried chicken chain in the countries we visit. We'd already been to Pollo Campestre, which José loved because it tasted like Pollo Campero's recipe from the 80s; I found it salty, but not bad, although it was also 9 p.m. chicken, fresh out of the fryer, haha.

At Tip-Top our plan was two pieces each: one grilled and one "rostizado", or fried (I don't get why they call it rostizado if it's fried, but to each their own, HAHA; maybe I'm the one who doesn't know). We explained to the lady that we wanted one fried piece, one grilled piece, fries and water each, and walked out with a bill of 1,300 córdobas, more than the steak we'd had the day before in León. Strange, because the two-piece combo was under 400 córdobas, but since we didn't know how to order, once again we paid extra to learn.

In the end they handed us two trays full of chicken, with beans, pico de gallo, tortillas, a drink and the famous cabbage salad. Of course we finished it all, but it was tough. We went back to the hotel to rest and slept until nightfall, which we spent in the pool for about 3 hours, hahahaha.

People have always told me I have an impressive ability for introspection and for noticing subtle changes around me. I've been discovering that it's a response to things from childhood; not necessarily violence, but situations that leave a mark. I've always loved pools: it's one of the things I remember from when we were little, when my mom took us swimming at Club Aurora, maybe not every weekend, but pretty often. I usually float, holding my breath, like a starfish, and while I float I relax and start connecting with my own mind. Everything José and I have done this week has been an almost chaotic back-and-forth. There's nothing wrong with that, since we're used to high stress, but I hadn't had time to process it.

Floating, trying not to drown, I remembered everyone's photos at the cemetery and remembered my own loved ones who have passed: my grandfather Armando, my brother Hanz, my grandmother Odilia. I'm blessed to still have many of my elders alive, but I know it won't be forever. I remembered that my brother Hanz drowned, 10 years ago. I remembered the saying that fear weighs you down, and I started thinking that we were never raised around fear: we weren't afraid of anything, literally anything.

I remember once we were walking through an ecological park in the town where we grew up and a snake appeared. While everyone stepped back, the two of us stood there watching it in awe, mesmerized by its movement, careful not to bother it, but never afraid. I put my head under the water trying to imagine what my brother might have felt: the fear of not seeing the bottom of the lake, of having nothing to hold on to and, hardest of all, the moment he probably called for help and nobody was close enough to help him right away.

When my brother died, something stayed with me. After so many movies, series and anime, you learn that when someone leaves this life there's always someone to blame, and all the pain gets redirected toward that person or that structure; that keeps you company and makes the pain smaller, because at least there's someone to blame. But when the one to blame is the very person who died, what do you do? Life taught me, the hard way, that there isn't always someone to blame, and that sometimes you just have to let go.

I remembered my grandfather Armando, on my dad's side, who couldn't see all the good and the bad I've done in the years he's been gone; I'm sure having him close would have been a great comfort, and he'd be proud. And my grandmother Odilia, my mom's mother, who, even though we didn't get along in her last years, was always proud of what her grandchildren did.

I saw where I was standing: in Nicaragua, in the same hotel where I stayed in 2019 for an event. But I realized it wasn't the same. Heraclitus's paradox is true: you never step into the same river twice, because neither the river nor you are the same. This pool was the same, but I wasn't, nor was the situation, nor the water. I felt proud of everything that has changed in these years, and I convinced myself to keep going on this journey.

We went to dinner and then to bed. Apparently I swallowed a lot of pool water, because my stomach hurt. Hoping not to wake up sick, I lay down and slept deeply.
